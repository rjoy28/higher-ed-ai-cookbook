RCU Access Review

Who can do what in Riverside Christian University's student information system, set against who works there and in what job. Nobody at RCU keeps a list of what each job should hold. This page proposes one from the access people actually have, then lists everything that doesn't fit, for a person to review.

What counts as a role?

A role is proposed for each job with enough people in it: the permissions most of them hold. Raise the threshold and the role gets narrower. Then ordinary access for the job starts to look extra.

The page proposes and flags. It never removes access. Some flags settle themselves once someone checks: a person who left needs their account closed. Others need someone who knows the office. Those are marked A person decides.

Each question on this page has one named source, and someone keeps it. The HR system says who works here and in what job. The SIS says what each account can do. When they disagree, the HR system is the record, and the SIS is what needs fixing. One question has no source at all: what each job should hold. That is the gap the proposed roles start to fill.

Behind the page

In the recipe, an AI assistant reads the SIS security user list and the HR export, proposes a role for each job from the access its holders share, and lists every account that doesn't fit, with the evidence: an earlier job, a grant, a separation date, a last sign-in. It never removes access. The offices adopt or correct the roles, and a person closes each item on the list.

It applies the fact source per control axis pattern to internal control: the HR record answers who should have access, and the security user list answers who does. The drift lives between them. The Systems Atlas shows why: staff sign in to the SIS with local passwords, so the nightly job that closes accounts never reaches it.
